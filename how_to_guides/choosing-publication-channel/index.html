    Choosing a journal or conference for publication

    This guide provides practical support for choosing a suitable journal or conference for a research manuscript.

    Your choice of publication channel should be based on the topic, intended audience and type of results in your research. You should also investigate whether the channel meets relevant requirements for example regarding open access, research data, licences and publication ethics.

    The guide focuses on scholarly journals, but many of the steps are also relevant when choosing a conference and publishing in conference proceedings, especially in engineering and computer science.

    Do not choose based on one indicator only

    A journal's Impact Factor, CiteScore or other indicators can provide context, but they cannot on their own determine whether a publication channel is suitable for your manuscript. Only compare journals within relevant and closely related subject areas. Always assess topical fit, intended audience, publishing practices and open-science requirements first.

    Start with your research and audience

    A good publication channel is a place where the right readers can find, use and discuss your results. Start by considering which scholarly conversation you want to contribute to and who should be able to use the results.

    It can be useful to begin with journals and conferences where your research group or department has published before. There are often good reasons why established channels are used repeatedly: they may be relevant forums for the topic, have a recognised readership in the field and be well known to colleagues and supervisors.

    Following an established publishing tradition should not, however, be an automatic choice. Another publication channel may be more suitable if the research is interdisciplinary, addresses a new audience or has specific requirements concerning open access, research data or software.

    Questions to start with:

    • Which researchers, professional groups or other audiences do you want to reach?
    • What subject, method and type of data does the manuscript concern?
    • Is it an original research article, review article, methods article, short communication, data article, software paper or another publication type?
    • Are there specific requirements from co-authors, supervisors, the project or the research funder?
    • Is a journal, conference, preprint, or a combination of these most appropriate for the results?

    Find possible publication channels

    Start by creating a list of several potential journals or conferences. It is useful to have alternatives before submitting a manuscript, since rejection does not necessarily mean that the work lacks quality or relevance.

    A simple way to find candidates is to investigate where relevant and recently published work in your field has appeared. Use reference lists, citing articles and literature searches in databases relevant to your discipline.

    You can also use tools that suggest journals based on a title, abstract and keywords:

    • Web of Science Master Journal List includes a manuscript matching function.
    • JANE can be used for biomedical subjects.
    • B!SON suggests suitable open access journals using semantic and bibliometric methods.
    • Many publishers offer their own journal matching tools, including Elsevier, IEEE, Springer Nature, Taylor & Francis and Wiley.

    Matching tools provide suggestions, not decisions

    Results from a journal matching tool should always be checked manually. These tools can identify topically similar publications, but they cannot determine whether your manuscript meets the journal's requirements or fits its actual readership.

    Check that your manuscript fits

    Always read the journal's or conference's information before selecting a publication channel. The most important sources are Aims and scope and Instructions for Authors.

    Also read several recently published articles. These often provide a better picture of what the channel actually publishes than the formal description alone.

    Check in particular:

    • Whether the topic, research question and method fit the channel's scope.
    • Whether the journal or conference accepts the type of article you have written.
    • Requirements regarding language, length, reference style, figures, tables and supplementary material.
    • Whether there are requirements to share data, software, protocols or other research materials.
    • Whether preprints are allowed.
    • Whether there are specific requirements regarding ethical approval, informed consent, competing interests or contributor roles.

    When you later write a cover letter to the editor, you can clearly explain why your manuscript fits the journal's scope and is relevant to its readers.

    Check indexing and visibility

    When possible, choose a publication channel that is visible in the databases and information systems used by relevant readers in your field.

    For many disciplines, Scopus and the Web of Science Master Journal List are relevant sources for checking journal indexing. In some fields, however, disciplinary databases are more important, for example Inspec, Compendex, MEDLINE, Chemical Abstracts, MathSciNet, zbMATH, ERIC or GeoRef.

    Verify information in the source database

    A journal's website may state that it is indexed in a particular database. If needed, verify this information in the database's own source list. Be especially cautious if a journal uses vague or misleading statements about indexing or journal indicators.

    Compare indicators within your subject area

    Journal indicators can be used as one of several sources of information when comparing journals with similar topical focus. Examples include Journal Impact Factor (JIF), Journal Citation Indicator (JCI), CiteScore, SJR and SNIP.

    • Journal Citation Reports includes JIF and JCI, based on data from Web of Science.
    • Scopus Sources provides CiteScore, SJR and SNIP.
    • SCImago Journal & Country Rank provides SJR and subject categories based on Scopus data.
    • CWTS Journal Indicators provides SNIP.
    • Norwegian Register for Scientific Journals, Series and Publishers provides levels for publication channels assessed by disciplinary expert panels.

    Do not compare indicators across entirely different subject areas. Citation patterns vary substantially between fields, and a value that is high in one field may be average or low in another.

    Field-normalised indicators

    JCI, SJR and SNIP are designed to account for differences between subject areas. JIF and CiteScore are not field-normalised. Even field-normalised indicators should be used with caution and alongside qualitative assessments of the channel's relevance and publishing practices.

    Check open access, licences and costs

    Investigate open access options before selecting a journal. Many research funders require scholarly publications to be openly available, and their requirements may also cover licences, embargoes and self-archiving.

    Check:

    • Whether the journal is fully open access, hybrid or subscription-based.
    • Whether an article processing charge, APC, applies and whether the cost can be covered by an institutional agreement or project funding.
    • Which licence is offered or required, for example a Creative Commons licence.
    • Whether you can self-archive a preprint or accepted manuscript version in an open repository.
    • Whether the journal meets the requirements of your research funder.

    KTH Library provides current information about open access publishing and costs that may be covered through KTH agreements:

    • Publish Open Access – we cover your costs
    • KTH policy for publishing
    • cOAlition S Journal Checker Tool
    • Jisc Open Policy Finder for information about self-archiving and rights.

    Check costs before submission

    APCs and other fees may vary between journals and agreements. A journal may be open access without its APC being covered by KTH. Therefore, check that the journal is included in a relevant agreement and that you meet the agreement's eligibility conditions before submitting your manuscript.

    Check requirements for research data and software

    Journals and publishers may have specific requirements for data availability statements, sharing of data and software, deposit in repositories, or citation of datasets and software.

    Check these requirements before selecting a publication channel. This is particularly important if data contain personal information, are protected by confidentiality or agreements, are covered by intellectual-property rights, or cannot otherwise be openly shared.

    You should be able to explain:

    • Which data, software and other materials support the reported results.
    • Whether, where and how the materials can be shared, for example in a disciplinary repository or the KTH Data Repository.
    • Which licence applies to the material.
    • Why some data cannot be openly shared, and whether metadata or controlled access can be provided instead.

    Base your planning on the project's data management plan and check requirements from both the research funder and the journal. KTH's guidance on data management plans is a suitable starting point.

    Assess peer review, timelines and editorial transparency

    Publication time, likelihood of acceptance and the peer-review process can be important considerations, especially when an article needs to be published before a doctoral thesis, project reporting or another time-sensitive milestone.

    Look for information about:

    • Average time from submission to first decision.
    • Time from acceptance to publication.
    • Acceptance rate, if it is reported.
    • Type of peer review, for example single-anonymised, double-anonymised or open peer review.
    • Whether preprints may be shared, review reports may be published or reviewers may be suggested.
    • The editorial board, contact information and responsibility for publication ethics.

    The journal's own website and author instructions are the most important sources. Transpose may provide information on some journals' policies for peer review, co-reviewing and preprints. SciRev contains researcher-reported experiences of review processes.

    Timeline information is approximate

    Information about review times and acceptance rates may be incomplete, outdated or dependent on the manuscript's topic and quality. Use it as guidance rather than a guarantee.

    Avoid questionable publication channels

    Be attentive to journals and publishers with unclear or misleading publishing practices. Questionable channels may use aggressive invitations, promise unrealistically rapid publication, or make misleading claims about indexing and indicators.

    Warning signs may include:

    • The editorial board, contact information or ownership is difficult to identify.
    • The peer-review process is unclear or implausibly fast.
    • Fees are hidden or only disclosed after submission.
    • The journal claims indexing that cannot be confirmed in the source database.
    • The journal name or website is deceptively similar to that of an established journal.
    • Email invitations are irrelevant to your field or contain obvious errors.

    See also Karolinska Institutet University's guidance on questionable journals.

    Document and justify your decision

    Before submitting, you should be able to explain why the chosen publication channel is appropriate. A brief justification can be useful in discussions with co-authors and supervisors, and helps you write a relevant cover letter to the editor.

    For example:

    We choose [journal or conference name] because it reaches a relevant audience in [subject area] and publishes work on [topic or method]. The channel accepts [publication type], is visible in [relevant databases] and meets our requirements regarding [open access, data, software or licence]. We have also checked that the publishing process and costs are reasonable for the project. If the manuscript is not accepted, [alternative channel] is a possible next choice.

    Consider making a simple comparison of two to four candidates before you decide. Document topical fit, audience, indexing, open access options, cost, data and software requirements, indicators and expected publication timeline.

    This guide summarises the most important steps. You can always contact KTH Library for support with publishing strategy, open access, research data and bibliometric questions.
